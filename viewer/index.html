REE (e·sios): what the grid operator really did

Red Eléctrica's own data for the stages after the market, from its e·sios platform: redispatch for grid security and the balancing markets. These are the real counterparts of the model's last two stages (BAL and AC redispatch). Spanish peninsula, 2024; the data is downloaded with esios_download/fetch_esios.py (model repo) and built into reference/esios_2024.js by tools/build_esios.py.

Redispatch: model vs what REE really did

How much the grid stage turned plants up and down, next to REE's own redispatch for grid security (e·sios). REE acts on the day-ahead programme and starts plants, mostly gas, where the grid needs them; the model acts on its balancing schedule.

Extra cost of redispatch

Why the difference?

Pooled to match the model's single redispatch stage: REE's technical constraints after the day-ahead market (phase I turns plants up or down for grid security, phase II rebalances) plus real-time constraints, Spanish peninsula, from e·sios (volumes 701–704, 720, 721; costs 1373–1376, 1723, 1724). REE pays what goes up its own bid including start-up costs, and refunds what goes down at its bid or the day-ahead price (P.O. 3.2, P.O. 14.4); the cost is the extra cost over the market, paid by consumers. Model: AC redispatch minus balancing over all units except the border flows, valued at the model's marginal costs; it includes grid losses, and failed AC hours have no value.

Balancing: the model's BAL stage vs REE's balancing markets

The model's balancing price next to REE's balancing energy prices, and how much balancing energy REE used. Spain has no single balancing price: RR (replacement reserve) is the hourly market closest to the model's BAL stage; aFRR is the automatic reserve.

Largest plants

Real vs model uses ENTSO-E's measured output per unit, matched to the model's plants in entsoe_unit_crosswalk.csv; plants ENTSO-E reports only in part are left out. Output and change are from the AC redispatch; "Redispatch change" is redispatch output minus balancing output, so it shows what the grid stage changed. Hydro includes pumped storage (pumping is not counted as output). Not shown: the model's industrial CHP, waste and mini-hydro blocks (spread over 100 substations, so not at real locations). Failed AC hours are left out of averages.

Trading margin: what a storage unit could earn on this run's prices

For every day, the best plan for one storage unit that charges in the cheapest hours and discharges in the most expensive ones, worked out on this run's model day-ahead price or, if you pick it, OMIE's real one for the same days. It is an upper bound for that price: the unit knows the whole day's prices in advance, does not move the price and trades the day-ahead only. Whether that margin pays for building the unit is on the Investment case tab.

Investment case: does the trading margin pay for building it?

The trading margin from the Trading margin tab, set against what the unit costs to build (CAPEX) and to run (OPEX) over its lifetime.

Where the costs come from. TES: PNNL's 2022 storage cost report (molten salt with an electric heater and a steam cycle, 2021 dollars at 0.924 €/$), without a new turbine since Soto de Ribera has one; refurbishing the old turbine is not included (fixed sum 0). Real check: Project Alba in Chile (AES Andes) turns a 560 MW coal plant into molten-salt storage for US$450 M, about two-thirds of this per kW out. Battery: still made-up placeholders. The trading margin of this run's year is assumed to repeat every year of the lifetime (changed by "Margin change" if set).

Money in or out so far, discounted to today: the investment in year 0, then each year's cash (trading margin − fixed OPEX − variable OPEX). Where it crosses zero, the investment is paid back.
